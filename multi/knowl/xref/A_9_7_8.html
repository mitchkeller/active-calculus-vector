Exercise 14.

The left side of Figure 10.2.10 shows the curve described by the vector-valued function \(\vr\) defined by
\begin{equation*} \vr(t) = \left\langle 2t-\frac12 t^2 + 1,t-1\right\rangle. \end{equation*}
🔗

(a)

Find the object’s velocity \(\vv(t)\text{.}\)
🔗
Solution.
Velocity is the derivative of position, so
\begin{equation*} \vv(t) = \vr'(t)= \langle 2-t, 1 \rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}

(b)

Find the object’s acceleration \(\va(t)\text{.}\)
🔗
Solution.
Acceleration is the derivative of velocity, so
\begin{equation*} \va(t) = \vv'(t)= \langle -1, 0 \rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}

(d)

Recall that the speed is \(\vecmag{\vv} = \sqrt{\vv\cdot\vv}\text{.}\) Find the object’s speed and graph it as a function of time \(t\) on the right of Figure 10.2.10. When is the object’s speed the slowest? When is the speed increasing? When is it decreasing?
🔗
Solution.
Speed is the magnitude of the velocity, so the speed of the object at time \(t\) is
\begin{equation*} s(t) = \sqrt{(2-t)^2+1} \end{equation*}
The speed of the object is at its least when \(t=2\text{.}\) The speed is increasing on the interval \((2,4)\) and decreasing on the interval \((-4,2)\text{.}\)

(e)

What seems to be true about the angle between \(\vv\) and \(\va\) when the speed is at a minimum? What is the angle between \(\vv\) and \(\va\) when the speed is increasing? when the speed is decreasing?

(f)

Since the square root is an increasing function, we see that the speed increases precisely when \(\vv\cdot\vv\) is increasing. Use the product rule for the dot product to express \(\frac{d}{dt}(\vv\cdot\vv)\) in terms of the velocity \(\vv\) and acceleration \(\va\text{.}\) Use this to explain why the speed is increasing when \(\vv\cdot\va \gt 0\) and decreasing when \(\vv\cdot\va \lt 0\text{.}\) Compare this to part d.
🔗
Solution.
Using derivative properties we have
\begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dt}(\vv\cdot\vv) = \vv \cdot \frac{d}{dt} \vv + \frac{d}{dt} \vv \cdot \vv = 2(\vv \cdot \va)\text{.} \end{equation*}
So the derivative of the speed is positive when \(\vv \cdot \va\) is positive and negative when \(\vv \cdot \va\) is negative. In other words, speed is increasing when \(\vv \cdot \va\) is positive and decreasing when \(\vv \cdot \va\) is negative. When \(t \lt 2\) in the position graph, the angle between \(\vv\) and \(\va\) is larger than \(90^{\circ}\) and so \(\vv \cdot \va \lt 0\text{.}\) When \(t \gt 2\) in the position graph, the angle between \(\vv\) and \(\va\) is less than \(90^{\circ}\) and so \(\vv \cdot \va \gt 0\text{.}\) Note that at the point when \(t=2\text{,}\) the vectors \(\vv\) and \(\va\) are orthogonal.

(g)

Show that the speed’s rate of change is
\begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dt}\vecmag{\vv(t)} = \comp_{\vv} \va. \end{equation*}
🔗
Solution.
In general, if \(\vr(t) = \langle x(t), y(t) \rangle\text{,}\) then \(\vv(t) = \langle x'(t), y'(t) \rangle\text{,}\) \(\va(t) = \langle x''(t), y''(t) \rangle\text{,}\) and the speed is \(s(t) = \sqrt{(x'(t))^2 + (y'(t))^2}\text{.}\) Recall that \(\comp_{\vv} \va = \frac{\va \cdot \vv}{\vecmag{\vv}}\text{,}\) so
\begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dt} s(t) = \frac{x'(t)x''(t) +y'(t)y''(t)}{\vecmag{\vv}} = \comp_{\vv} \va \end{equation*}
