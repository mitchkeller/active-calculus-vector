Preview Activity 11.10.1.

A shipping company places a restriction on packages so that the sum of the package’s length and girth must be at most 12 feet. (The girth is the perimeter of the smallest end of the package.) Our goal is to find the largest possible volume of a rectangular package with a square end that can be sent by this shipping company.
🔗
Let \(x\) be the length in feet of the side of one square end of the package and \(y\) be the length in feet of the package. This allows us to define a function that gives the volume of the package as \(f(x,y) = x^2y\) cubic feet. The girth of the package is \(4x\text{,}\) so we can define \(g(x,y)=4x+y\) to be the sum of the girth and length. The domain on which we wish to maximize \(f\) is all points for which \(x\geq 0\text{,}\) \(y\geq 0\text{,}\) and \(g(x,y)\leq 12\text{.}\) A moment of reflection should confirm that any package with \(g(x,y)\lt 12\) cannot maximize the volume, as increasing either \(x\) or \(y\) so that \(g(x,y)\) remains at most 12 will only lead to a larger volume. Thus, we say that we wish to maximize \(f(x,y) = x^2y\) subject to the constraint \(g(x,y) = 4x+y = 12\text{.}\)
🔗

(a)

Compute \(\nabla f\) and evaluate the gradient at the points \((1,8)\text{,}\) \((1.5,6)\text{,}\) \((2,4)\text{,}\) and \((2.5,2)\text{.}\)
🔗
Solution.
Computing the \(x\)-partial of the first component and the \(y\)-partial of the second component, we have \(\nabla f = \langle 2xy,x^2\rangle\text{.}\) Evaluation gives
\begin{align*} \nabla f(1,8) \amp= \langle 8,1\rangle \amp \nabla f(1.5,6) \amp = \langle 18,2.25\rangle\\ \nabla f(2,4) \amp= \langle 16,4\rangle \amp \nabla f(2.5,2) \amp= \langle 10,6.25\rangle\text{.} \end{align*}

(c)

Compute \(\grad g\text{.}\) Describe the geometric relationship between \(\nabla g\) and the line \(g(x,y) = 12\) plotted in the previous part and why they have this relationship.
🔗
Solution.
Computing partials, we have \(\nabla g=\langle 4,1\rangle\text{.}\) Because \(g(x,y)=12\) is a level curve of the function \(g(x,y)\text{,}\) we know that the gradient will be orthogonal to the line.

(d)

Recall that the gradient always points in the direction of steepest ascent. However, in this constrained optimization setting, the domain that we allowed to consider for \(f\) is only those points for which \(g(x,y)=12\text{.}\) Thus, we may not be able to move in the direction of the gradient while staying within the domain. Look back at the four points at which you sketched \(\nabla f\) in part b. For each point, identify which direction you could move along the line to increase the value of \(f\text{.}\) What is the relationship between \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) at a point where you cannot move along the line while increasing the value of \(f\text{?}\)
🔗
Hint.
For each of the points in the plot, you may find it helfpul to sketch \(\nabla g\) with its tail at the point.
🔗
🔗
Solution.
For \((1,8)\) and \((1.5,6)\text{,}\) move toward larger \(x\)-values. For \((2.5,2)\) move toward smaller \(x\)-values. Because \(\nabla f(2,4) = \langle 16,4\rangle = 4\langle 4,1\rangle = 4\nabla g(2,4)\text{,}\) the gradients are parallel at \((2,4)\) and it is not possible to increase the value of \(f\) while staying on the line.
